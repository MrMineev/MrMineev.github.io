\begin{theorem}[Four-base conic-pencil Evil]
\label{thm:evil}
Let \(\mathcal K\) be a nonsingular conic through
\(A,B,C,I,J,F\), and fix \(D\notin\mathcal K\).
As \(\mathcal H\) varies through \(A,I,J,D\), put
\[
 R=(\mathcal H\cap\mathcal K)\setminus\{A,I,J\},
 \quad X=(\mathcal H\cap AC)\setminus\{A\},
 \quad P=FR\cap BC.
\]
The lines \(PX\) pass through one fixed point
\(Z=Z(D)\). If \(\mathcal K=(ABC)\) and \(I,J\)
are the circular points at infinity, this
is the \(AC\)-half of Evil;
interchanging \(B,C\) gives the other half.
\end{theorem}
\diagram{sequel-evil}
\begin{proof}
The pencil parameter gives projectivities
to the remaining points \(X\in AC\) and
\(R\in\mathcal K\); projecting \(R\) from
\(F\) gives \(P\in BC\). These two point
rows agree at their common point \(C\):
the member through \(C\) gives \(R=X=P=C\).
Hence their projectivity is a perspectivity,
with center \(Z\).
\end{proof}

Exchanging \(B\) and \(C\) proves the existence of the
other Evil anchor point in exactly the same way.

\begin{theorem}[Conic-to-line anchor]
\label{thm:line}
In Theorem~\ref{thm:evil}, instead take a
fixed line \(\ell\) through the fourth
base point \(D\), and put
\(V=(\mathcal H\cap\ell)\setminus\{D\}\).
Then the lines \(RV\) pass through one
fixed point \(S\) \emph{on} \(\mathcal K\).
The point \(F\) plays no role in this reading.
\end{theorem}
\diagram{sequel-line}
\begin{proof}
The correspondence \(R\in\mathcal K
\leftrightarrow V\in\ell\) is projective.
At each of the two points of
\(\ell\cap\mathcal K\), both points
of the correspondence coincide.
Choose any third pair \(R_0,V_0\)
and let \(S\) be the other intersection
of \(R_0V_0\) with \(\mathcal K\).
Projection from \(S\) gives the same
three correspondences, hence all of them.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Residual line of a conic pencil]
\label{thm:residual}
Fix \(V,I,J,F\in\mathcal K\) and
\(D\notin\mathcal K\). For the pencil
\(\mathcal H\) through \(V,I,J,D\),
let \(U\) be its fourth point on
\(\mathcal K\), and let \(S_U\) be
its other intersection with the
line \(FU\). All \(S_U\) lie on
one fixed line \(L_{V,F}(D)\) through
\(D\); moreover \(U\mapsto S_U\)
is projective.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write matching conic and line pencils
\(h_{s,t}=s h_0+t h_1\) and
\(\ell_{s,t}=s\ell_0+t\ell_1=FU\).
Eliminating \((s:t)\) gives the cubic
\(h_0\ell_1-h_1\ell_0=0\).
It contains every \(U\in\mathcal K\),
so it factors as \(\mathcal K\cup L\).
The fourth base point \(D\) lies on
the cubic but not on \(\mathcal K\),
so \(D\in L\); every other intersection
\(S_U\) lies on \(L\).
Restriction of the conic pencil to \(L\),
with fixed root \(D\), makes \(S_U\)
projective in its parameter.
\end{proof}
\diagram{sequel-pascal}

With \(I,J\) circular, the circles
\((UVD)\) are this pencil as
\(U\) varies on \(\Gamma\).
The \href{https://artofproblemsolving.com/community/u1159306h3745146p36855351}
{Evil characterization} puts the
conjugate for \emph{any} triangle
\(UVW\) on \(DS_U=L_{V,F}(D)\):
the third vertex \(W\) is irrelevant.
Equivalently,
\[
 \angle(DV,L_{V,F}(D))
                 =\angle VUF\pmod{180^\circ},
\]
which depends only on chord \(VF\).
Taking \(V=A,B,C\) gives the
three lines through \(D\) containing
the six conjugates in the handout.

\begin{theorem}[\href{https://artofproblemsolving.com/community/c6h287213p1552306}
{Irish Mathematical Olympiad 2007, Paper 1 Problem 3}]
The point \(P\) is a fixed point on a circle and \(Q\)
is a fixed point on a line. The point \(R\) is a variable
point on the circle such that \(P,Q,\) and \(R\) are not
collinear. The circle through \(P,Q,\) and \(R\) meets
the line again at \(V\). Show that the line \(VR\) passes
through a fixed point.
\end{theorem}
\diagram[0.38]{sequel-irish}

\begin{proof}
Call the fixed circle \(\Gamma\) and the fixed line
\(\ell\). The circles \((PQR)\) form the pencil through
\(P,Q,I,J\), where \(I,J\) are circular. It shares
\(P,I,J\) with \(\Gamma\), while \(Q\in\ell\)
is its fourth base point. Theorem~\ref{thm:line}
gives the fixed point of \(VR\) on \(\Gamma\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[USEMO 2025 Day 1 P2, Iman Maghsoudi]
Let \(ABC\) be a triangle with circumcircle
\(\Gamma\), and let \(P\) vary inside \(ABC\).
Set \(X=CP\cap AB\), \(Y=BP\cap AC\),
\(Q=(AXY)\cap\Gamma\setminus\{A\}\),
and \(K=AP\cap\Gamma\setminus\{A\}\).
The circumcircles \((QPK)\) have a
common radical center.
\end{theorem}
\diagram[0.30]{usemo-config}

\begin{proof}
Let \(M\) be the midpoint of \(BC\).
We prove that every defined circle
\((QPK)\) has power \(MB^2\) at \(M\).
Fix \(K\) off the \(A\)-median
and let \(P\) move on \(AK\).
Give \(AK\) a projective parameter
\(t\) with \(AK\cap BC\) at \(0\)
and \(A\) at \(\infty\).
Projecting from \(C,B\) and
inverting about \(A\) gives
\(X^*=(1+\alpha t)B^*\),
\(Y^*=(1+\beta t)C^*\)
for fixed \(\alpha\ne\beta\).
Since \(Q^*=X^*Y^*\cap B^*C^*\),
an affine intercept calculation gives
\[
 Q^*=\frac{\beta(1+\alpha t)}{\beta-\alpha}B^*
       -\frac{\alpha(1+\beta t)}{\beta-\alpha}C^*.
                                                        \tag{4}
\]
Thus \(P\mapsto Q\in\Gamma\) is
projective, with \(P=A\mapsto Q=A\)
as \(t\to\infty\).

Now invert about \(K\). The images
of \(P\in AK\) and \(Q\in\Gamma\)
lie on two lines and correspond
projectively. They coincide at
their common point \(A^\dagger\)
when \(P=Q=A\). Hence their
joining lines have a fixed center;
inverting back, all circles
\((QPK)\) pass through a fixed
second point \(R_K\).
\end{proof}
\diagram[0.24]{usemo-inverted}

\noindent
To locate \(R_K\), the two limiting
values \(t=-1/\alpha,-1/\beta\) in (4)
give \(Q=C,B\), respectively.
Let \(P_C\) lie on \(AK\) and on
the line through \(C\) parallel
to \(AB\); similarly let
\(P_B\in AK\) lie on the line
through \(B\) parallel to \(AC\).
The two limiting circles are
\[
              \Omega_C=(CP_CK),
              \qquad\Omega_B=(BP_BK).
\]
By the tangent--chord theorem,
\(\Omega_C\) is tangent to \(BC\)
at \(C\), because
\(\angle CP_CK=\angle BAK=\angle BCK\);
symmetrically, \(\Omega_B\)
is tangent to \(BC\) at \(B\).
Their other intersection is \(R_K\).
The next diagram takes another generic
position of \(K\) so both limiting circles
are visible.
\diagram[0.32]{usemo-limits}

\noindent
Their powers at \(M\) are
\(\operatorname{Pow}_{\Omega_C}(M)=MC^2
 =MB^2=\operatorname{Pow}_{\Omega_B}(M)\).
Thus \(M\) lies on their radical
axis \(KR_K\), giving
\(MK\cdot MR_K=MB^2\).
Every \((QPK)\) contains \(K,R_K\),
so its power at \(M\) is \(MB^2\),
independent of both \(P\) and \(K\).
The exceptional limiting positions
follow by continuity.

When \(P\) lies on the \(A\)-median,
\(\alpha=\beta\): \((AXY)\) is homothetic
to \(\Gamma\) about \(A\) and tangent
there, so \(Q=A\) and \(A,P,K\)
are collinear. The original
\((QPK)\) is then undefined as
an ordinary circle.

\noindent\textbf{Reusable three-position criterion.}
More generally, fix \(K\) off the
\(A\)-median. Any projectivity
\(\phi:AK\to\Gamma\) with
\(\phi(A)=A,\ \phi(P_C)=C,\ \phi(P_B)=B\)
has the same conclusion:
\(\operatorname{Pow}_{(K\,P\,\phi(P))}(M)=MB^2\).
The proof above uses no other properties
of how \(\phi\) was constructed.
