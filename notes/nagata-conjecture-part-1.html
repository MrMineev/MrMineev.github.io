Nagata Conjecture: Part 1

Algebraic Geometry · 🗓️ November 22, 2026

My notes from reading Jenia Tevelev's exposition (arXiv:2610.30050) of the proof of the Nagata conjecture announced by OpenAI. I am not an expert in algebraic geometry, and I wrote these to understand the proof myself, with help from AI along the way. There are likely mistakes; corrections are very welcome.

§1 Introduction

This text will be focused on the Nagata Conjecture stated below,

Theorem 1  (Nagata Conjecture)

For very general points \(p_1, \ldots, p_r \in \mathbb{P}^2\) (\(r \geq 10\)), every nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree \(d\) vanishing to order at least \(m_i \geq 1\) at each \(p_i\), satisfies, \[ d > \left( \sum_{i = 1}^{r} m_i\right) / \sqrt{r} \] the sum \(\sum_{i = 1}^{r} m_i\) will be denoted by \(M\).

The term very general refers to a configuration in \((\mathbb{P}^2)^r \setminus S\), where \(S\) is some countable union of proper Zariski closed sets. A special case of this conjecture is its homogeneous version.

Theorem 2  (Homogeneous Nagata Conjecture)

For very general points \(p_1, \ldots, p_r \in \mathbb{P}^2\) (\(r \geq 10\)), every nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree \(d\) vanishing to order at least \(m\) at each \(p_i\), satisfies, \[ d > m \sqrt{r} \]

Proposition 1

The Nagata Conjecture and its homogeneous version are equivalent.

Proof. Let \(B_{d, \mathcal{M}} \subset (\mathbb{P}^2)^r\) (where \(\mathcal{M}\) denotes \(\{m_i\}\)) denote the set of \(r\) points such that there exists some non-zero form of degree \(d\) vanishing to order at least \(m_i\) at each point \(p_i\) (it is shown in Proposition 2) that this set is Zariski closed. Let us define very general in the Nagata conjecture to mean in \((\mathbb{P}^2)^r \setminus \left( S \cup \bigcup \left\{ B_{d, \mathcal{M}} : B_{d, \mathcal{M}} \neq (\mathbb{P}^2)^r \right\} \right)\) (where \(S\) is the exceptional set of the Homogeneous Nagata Conjecture). Thus, if \(F\) is some non-zero form vanishing (with the conditions described in the theorem) at points \(p_1, \ldots, p_r\) (some very general points) with multiplicities \(m_1, \ldots, m_r\), then \(B_{d, \mathcal{M}}\) must be everything. Thus, one can choose \(F_i\) such that it vanishes at \(p_j\) to order at least \(m_{i + j}\). Observe that then \(F_0 F_1 \ldots F_{r - 1}\) is a form that vanishes at every point to order at least \(M\) and thus by Theorem 2) it will follow that \(dr > M \sqrt{r}\) which is equivalent to the desired result!

Let \(B_{d, m} \subset (\mathbb{P}^2)^r\) denote the set of \(r\) points such that there exists some non-zero form of degree \(d\) vanishing to order at least \(m\) at every point, it will be shown in a moment that this set is Zariski closed. If \(d \leq m \sqrt{r}\), then by exhibiting some \(T \not \in B_{d, m}\) it will imply that \(B_{d, m}\) is a proper closed subset of \((\mathbb{P}^2)^r\) and consequently a very general position of points will avoid \(B_{d, m}\) which will imply the Nagata conjecture.

Theorem 3  (Main Theorem of Elimination Theory)

For any variety \(Y\) the projection map \(\pi : Y \times \mathbb{P}^n \rightarrow Y\) is closed, i.e. closed Zariski sets go to closed Zariski sets.

Proposition 2

\(B_{d, m}\) is a Zariski closed set

Proof. Consider the set, \[ Z = \{[F], P_1, \ldots, P_r \mid \mult F_{P_i} \geq m \text{ for all } i\} \] where \(Z \subset \mathbb{P}^N \times (\mathbb{P}^2)^r = \mathcal{A}\) (where \(N\) denotes all the monomials of a homogeneous degree \(d\) polynomial), since all the conditions are polynomial equations in the coefficients of \(F\) and the coordinates of the \(P_i\) it must be that \(Z\) is closed in \(\mathcal{A}\). By Theorem 3 this implies that \(\pi(Z) = B_{d, m}\) is closed!

A simple decorative trick is to observe that if \(d \leq m \sqrt{r}\) and \(F\) is some non-zero form satisfying the above conditions, then \(F^3\) has degree \(3d\) and the multiplicity at each point is \(3m\), thus giving a form with \(3d \leq 3m \sqrt{r}\). Thus, it is enough to show the theorem for all forms of degree multiples of \(3\) (more generally the same works for any multiples of \(k\), but this proof will use \(3\)).

§2 Limits and Collisions

Let us choose a point \(P = (\xi, \eta)\) (some holomorphic coordinates), then let us have \(r\) points \(P_i = (sc_i, 0)\) where \(c_i\) are distinct complex numbers and small enough \(s\). Let, \[ I_s = \{F: \mult_{P_i} F \geq m \text{ for } i \leq r \} \] and define \(p_s(w) = \prod_{i = 1}^{r} (w - sc_i)\). Let us rewrite \(F\) using the holomorphic coordinates as \(F = \sum_{a \geq 0} f_a(\xi) \eta^a\) (where \(f_a(\xi)\) is some power series in \(\xi\)). The condition that \(F \in I_s\) is equivalent to \(f_{a}(\xi)\) vanishing to order at least \(m - a\) for \(0 \leq a \leq m - 1\) at \(\xi = sc_i\), thus \(p_s(\xi)^{m - a} \mid f_a(\xi)\), which gives the alternative definition (for \(s \neq 0\)) of \(I_s\) as, \[ I_s = \left\{ F = \sum_{a \geq 0} f_a(\xi) \eta^a : p_s(\xi)^{m - a} \mid f_a(\xi) \right\} \] which now can be extended to the case \(s = 0\) that is going to be of importance, \[ I_0 = \left\{ F = \sum_{a \geq 0} f_a(\xi) \eta^a : \xi^{r(m - a)} \mid f_a(\xi) \right\} = (\xi^r, \eta)^m \] Let \(V_d\) denote the vector space of homogeneous degree \(d\) forms. I will slightly abuse notation when writing \(V_d \cap I_s\) it will be assumed that the forms are dehomogenized by dividing by some power of some linear form.

Theorem 4  (Collision)

Given a point \(P = (\xi, \eta)\) with \(V_d \cap (\xi^r, \eta)^m = \{0\}\), for all sufficiently small \(s \neq 0\) it holds that \(V_d \cap I_s = \{0\}\).

Proof. Let \(F = \sum_{a \geq 0} f_a(\xi) \eta^a \in V_d\), then \(p_{s, a}(w)\) denote the remainder of \(f_a(w)\) from division by \(p_s(w)^{m - a}\), then consider the following map, \[ R_s : V_d \longrightarrow \bigoplus_{a = 0}^{m - 1} \mathbb{C}[\xi]_{\lt r(m - a)}, \text{ where } F \longmapsto (p_{s, a})_{0 \leq a \leq m - 1} \] observe that \(\ker R_s = I_s \cap V_d\). Suppose the theorem is false, then let \(F_n\) be a sequence of non-zero forms in \(\ker R_{s_n}\) where \(s_n \rightarrow 0\). Let us fix some norm \(\lVert\cdot\rVert\) (which exists since \(V_d\) is a vector space over \(\mathbb{C}\)) on \(V_d\), then scale all the forms so that \(\lVert F_n \rVert = 1\), then since the unit sphere is compact (since \(V_d\) is finite-dimensional) we may choose a subsequence \(\{k_i\}\) by Bolzano–Weierstrass so that the limit \(F = \lim_{i \rightarrow \infty} F_{k_i}\) makes sense, then by continuity of \(R_s\) in \(s\) it must be that, \[ R_0 (F) = \lim_{i \rightarrow \infty} R_{s_{k_i}}(F_{k_i}) = 0 \] which contradicts \(\ker R_0\) being \(\{0\}\) since \(F \neq 0\) (since \(\lVert F \rVert = 1\))!

Thus, one needs to only construct \(P = (\xi, \eta)\) with \(V_d \cap (\xi^r, \eta)^m = \{0\}\) and then there will be some construction of \(r\) distinct points not in \(B_{d, m}\) which as noted earlier would imply the Nagata conjecture.
